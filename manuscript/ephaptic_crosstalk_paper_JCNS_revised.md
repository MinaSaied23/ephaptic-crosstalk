**Abstract:** Dynamic mechanical allodynia is hypothesized to involve non-synaptic (“ephaptic”) cross-excitation between myelinated Aβ mechanoreceptive afferents and unmyelinated C-fiber nociceptors at sites of focal demyelination or nerve compression. We present a closed-loop, one-dimensional core-conductor model coupling a Chiu-Ritchie-Rogart-Stagg-Sweeney (CRRSS) Aβ axon to a C-fiber through a shared, restricted extracellular cleft, using algebraic elimination of capacitive currents. C-fibers used classical Hodgkin-Huxley kinetics (Phase 1) or literature-derived Nav1.8/Nav1.9 kinetics (Phase 2, robustness check). Single-fiber-pair crosstalk remained subthreshold under single-pulse stimulation in both models (≤1.325 mV, Phase 1; ≤1.76 mV, Phase 2) across cleft widths of 20 nm to 5 μm. In a bundle of $n \in [1,50]$ synchronized Aβ fibers, return currents produced saturating subthreshold depolarization within 5 ms (+6.2 mV, Phase 1; +10.4 mV, Phase 2, at $n=50$), limited by axoplasmic shunting; a 1.5-ms onset-dispersion window attenuated it by 92.9–93.8%. In the ungrounded full-length model, a 100-Hz train produced a propagated C-fiber action potential ($\approx$ 0.41 m/s) during the second pulse interval, but it was initiated by the first pulse at the sealed stimulation boundary and delayed by slow conduction; a single pulse yields the same spike, so it does not demonstrate temporal summation. In a grounded interior-lesion control, endogenous crosstalk evoked no action potentials in either phase, including under 800-Hz trains. Isolated Aβ conduction velocity (41.03 m/s) and event classifications were robust to spatial refinement, though the single-pulse $n=25$ peak amplitude was not fully timestep-converged. Here, ephaptic coupling produced subthreshold depolarization but no threshold crossing without a boundary-initiated spike.

**Keywords:** ephaptic coupling, core-conductor model, neuropathic pain, dynamic mechanical allodynia, Nav1.8/Nav1.9 kinetics, spatial summation, temporal summation, Chiu-Ritchie-Rogart-Stagg-Sweeney kinetics

## 1 Introduction

Dynamic mechanical allodynia—pain evoked by light, moving tactile stimuli such as light brushing or the contact of clothing—is a debilitating hallmark of peripheral neuropathic pain conditions, including painful diabetic neuropathy (Asmedi et al., 2018), post-herpetic neuralgia, and nerve entrapment syndromes such as trigeminal neuralgia (Rappaport & Devor, 1994). A prominent biophysical hypothesis holds that at sites of focal demyelination, chronic mechanical compression, or traumatic nerve injury, the normally independent electrical activity of large myelinated Aβ mechanoreceptive afferents and small unmyelinated C-fiber nociceptors can become directly coupled through their shared, pathologically restricted extracellular space. This non-synaptic electrical interaction, termed "ephaptic" coupling, was first characterized biophysically by Arvanitaki (1942) and by Katz and Schmitt (1940). Experimental evidence for cross-excitation between afferent classes has since been documented in mammalian dorsal root ganglia (Amir & Devor, 2000; Devor & Wall, 1990) and in chronically injured peripheral nerve trunks (Rasminsky, 1980; Seltzer & Devor, 1979). Under this hypothesis, low-threshold tactile activity conducted by Aβ fibers could directly evoke ectopic action potentials in neighboring nociceptive axons, providing a peripheral mechanism for tactile allodynia that operates upstream of, or in concert with, central sensitization.

Computational investigations of ephaptic coupling in nervous tissue have traditionally followed two distinct approaches: open-loop forward models, in which the extracellular field generated by an active fiber is imposed as an external driving potential on an adjacent passive or excitable cable without reciprocal feedback (Goldwyn & Rinzel, 2016; Lindén et al., 2014), and closed-loop formulations restricted to homogeneous fiber populations, such as bundles of myelinated axons or central white-matter tracts (Reutskiy et al., 2003; Schmidt et al., 2021; Schmidt & Knösche, 2022). Foundational analytical and numerical work by Reutskiy, Rossoni, and Tirozzi (2003) demonstrated that bundle geometry and inter-fiber packing profoundly modulate conduction velocity and recruitment thresholds in demyelinated nerve. More recently, generalized higher-dimensional formulations of interacting parallel axon networks have elucidated the complex dynamical dependencies of ephaptic crosstalk on spatial packing, boundary conditions, and coupling topologies (Chawla & Morgera, 2024; Chawla et al., 2021a, 2021b). Fully coupled Extracellular–Membrane–Intracellular (EMI) finite-element formulations have further advanced the field by resolving three-dimensional micro-domains around excitable cells (Jæger & Tveito, 2022, 2026). Nevertheless, closed-loop bidirectional coupling between physiologically distinct fiber classes—specifically a fast myelinated Aβ mechanoreceptor and an unmyelinated C-fiber nociceptor—remains incompletely characterized, particularly concerning whether multi-fiber spatial convergence or repetitive temporal trains can bridge the threshold gap where single-pair coupling fails.

This paper reports a closed-loop core-conductor model developed to evaluate this interaction systematically across two complementary modeling phases. Phase 1 establishes a baseline system coupling a Chiu-Ritchie-Rogart-Stagg-Sweeney (CRRSS) myelinated Aβ axon to a classical Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) C-fiber through a shared extracellular cleft. Using this baseline, we test whether single-pair crosstalk can trigger ectopic nociceptor spikes under single-pulse stimulation, tonic peripheral sensitization, or high-frequency temporal summation. Recognizing that classical HH kinetics lack the tetrodotoxin-resistant (TTX-R) conductances characteristic of mammalian nociceptors, Phase 2 replaces the classical Na⁺ current with literature-derived Nav1.8 and Nav1.9 kinetics (Akopian et al., 1996; Cummins et al., 1999; Klein et al., 2017). Phase 2 is designed not as an independent biological validation of Phase 1, but as a biological sensitivity and robustness check within the same one-dimensional core-conductor framework. We then extend Phase 2 to a multi-fiber bundle configuration in which an ensemble of synchronized or temporally dispersed Aβ fibers converges upon a shared extracellular compartment with one C-fiber.

We report that single-fiber-pair crosstalk remains strictly subthreshold under single-pulse stimulation across both channel models. In contrast, multi-fiber spatial summation produces substantial subthreshold depolarizations that asymptotically saturate due to parallel axoplasmic conductance shunting. Furthermore, in the ungrounded full-length model a 100-Hz train yields a propagated C-fiber action potential during the second pulse interval; because a single pulse produces the same spike when observed for long enough, we attribute it to boundary initiation and slow conduction rather than to demonstrated temporal summation, and a grounded interior-lesion control evoked no action potentials.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Coupled Core-Conductor Cable Equations

We consider two parallel axons of length $L = 10$ mm embedded in a shared, longitudinally restricted extracellular cleft with resistance per unit length $r_e$ ($\Omega/\text{m}$). Axon $1$ ($k=1$) represents the myelinated Aβ fiber, and axon $2$ ($k=2$) represents the unmyelinated C-fiber. For each axon, the transmembrane potential is defined as $v_{m,k}(z,t) = u_{i,k}(z,t) - u_e(z,t)$, where $u_{i,k}$ is the intracellular potential and $u_e$ is the shared extracellular cleft potential. The coupled core-conductor cable equations are given by:

$$\frac{\partial v_{m,k}}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{C_{m,k}} \left[ \frac{d_{\text{axon},k}}{4 \rho_i} \left( \frac{\partial^2 v_{m,k}}{\partial z^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u_e}{\partial z^2} \right) - I_{\text{ion},k} + I_{\text{stim},k} \right]$$

where $d_{\text{axon},k}$ is the inner axonal core diameter ($d_{\text{axon},1} = 0.7 D_1 = 7.0\,\mu\text{m}$ for the myelinated Aβ fiber with outer diameter $D_1 = 10\,\mu\text{m}$, representing an established $g$-ratio of $0.7$; and $d_{\text{axon},2} = d_2 = 1.0\,\mu\text{m}$ for the unmyelinated C-fiber). The parameter $\rho_i = 54.7\,\Omega\cdot\text{cm} = 0.547\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$ is the axoplasmic resistivity (Sweeney et al., 1987), $C_{m,k}$ is the specific membrane capacitance ($\text{F/m}^2$), $I_{\text{ion},k}$ is the total transmembrane ionic current density ($\text{A/m}^2$), and $I_{\text{stim},k}$ is the applied intracellular stimulation current density ($\text{A/m}^2$). All current densities and capacitances are defined per unit inner-axonal membrane area ($\pi d_{\text{axon},k}$), ensuring mathematical consistency with the core-conductor derivation of the axial diffusion term. Symbols, parameters, and SI units are defined in Table 3. Sealed-end (Neumann) boundary conditions are enforced at both cable ends ($z = 0$ and $z = L$):

$$\left. \frac{\partial v_{m,k}}{\partial z} \right|_{z=0, L} = 0, \quad \left. \frac{\partial u_e}{\partial z} \right|_{z=0, L} = 0$$

### 2.2 Membrane Kinetics and Conduction Properties

#### 2.2.1 Myelinated Aβ Fiber (CRRSS Kinetics)

The Aβ axon employs the Chiu-Ritchie-Rogart-Stagg-Sweeney (CRRSS) nodal kinetics (Chiu et al., 1979; Sweeney et al., 1987). Active ionic currents are localized to discrete physical nodes of Ranvier of fixed physical length $l_{\text{node}} = 1.0\,\mu\text{m}$, spaced at internodal intervals of $\Delta L = 1.0$ mm along the $10$ mm cable (10 nodes total, at $z = 0, 1, \dots, 9$ mm). To ensure that spatial grid refinement ($\Delta z$) does not alter the physical membrane area of the node, we apply a consistent homogenization scheme: for any computational compartment of width $\Delta z$, the fraction occupied by an active node is $f_{\text{node}} = l_{\text{node}} / \Delta z$ ($f_{\text{node}} \le 1$). The effective compartment capacitance and leak conductance are weighted accordingly:

$$C_{m,1} = f_{\text{node}} C_{m,\text{node}} + (1 - f_{\text{node}}) C_{m,\text{internode}}$$
$$g_{\text{leak},1} = f_{\text{node}} g_{\text{leak},\text{node}} + (1 - f_{\text{node}}) g_{\text{leak},\text{internode}}$$

Physical nodal parameters are: $\bar{g}_{\text{Na}} = 1445\,\text{mS/cm}^2 = 14450\,\text{S/m}^2$, $g_{\text{leak},\text{node}} = 128\,\text{mS/cm}^2 = 1280\,\text{S/m}^2$, $C_{m,\text{node}} = 2.0\,\mu\text{F/cm}^2$, $E_{\text{Na}} = +35.64$ mV, and $E_{\text{leak},1} = -80.0$ mV (Sweeney et al., 1987). Internodal compact myelin is parameterized with $C_{m,\text{internode}} = 0.005\,\mu\text{F/cm}^2$ and $g_{\text{leak},\text{internode}} = 0.006\,\text{mS/cm}^2$, with active Na⁺ channels absent. 

The gating rate equations follow the mammalian CRRSS formulation (Chiu et al., 1979; Sweeney et al., 1987) with corrected monotonic $h$-gate inactivation designations:

$$\alpha_m(v) = \frac{(97.0 + 0.363(v_{\text{mV}} + 80.0)) \times 10^3}{1.0 + \exp(-(v_{\text{mV}} + 49.0) / 5.3)}\,\text{s}^{-1}$$
$$\beta_m(v) = \alpha_m(v) \exp(-(v_{\text{mV}} + 56.2) / 4.17)$$
$$\beta_h(v) = \frac{15.6 \times 10^3}{1.0 + \exp(-(v_{\text{mV}} + 56.0) / 10.0)}\,\text{s}^{-1}$$
$$\alpha_h(v) = \beta_h(v) \exp(-(v_{\text{mV}} + 74.5) / 5.0)$$

where $v_{\text{mV}} = v_{m,1} \times 10^3$, corresponding to the classic deviation from resting potential $V = v_{\text{mV}} + 80.0\,\text{mV}$ in Chiu et al. (1979). The inactivation curve $h_\infty(v) = \alpha_h / (\alpha_h + \beta_h)$ decreases monotonically from $\approx 1.0$ at hyperpolarized potentials to $0.0$ upon depolarization, with half-inactivation at $V_{1/2} \approx -74.5$ mV. Rates are scaled to $37^\circ\text{C}$ using $Q_{10} = 3.0$ (Sweeney et al., 1987). Under this parameterization, an isolated Aβ fiber stimulated at node 0 ($100$ nA, $0.2$ ms) generates a brief, physiological saltatory action potential, reaching an absolute peak of approximately $+0.44$ mV from a resting potential of $-80.0$ mV, corresponding to an action-potential excursion of $80.44$ mV, conducting at **41.03 m/s** (measured via maximum upstroke $dV/dt$ between $4.0$ mm and $8.0$ mm), well within the established mammalian range ($30$–$60$ m/s).

#### 2.2.2 C-Fiber Membrane Models

The unmyelinated C-fiber ($d_2 = 1.0\,\mu\text{m}$, $C_{m,2} = 1.0\,\mu\text{F/cm}^2$) is modeled continuously along its length. In Phase 1, it follows classical Hodgkin-Huxley kinetics (Hodgkin & Huxley, 1952): $\bar{g}_{\text{Na}} = 120\,\text{mS/cm}^2$, $\bar{g}_{\text{K}} = 36\,\text{mS/cm}^2$, $g_{\text{leak}} = 0.3\,\text{mS/cm}^2$, $E_{\text{Na}} = +50.0$ mV, $E_{\text{K}} = -77.0$ mV, and $E_{\text{leak},2} = -54.4$ mV, resting stably at $-65.0$ mV. The delayed-rectifier $\text{K}^+$ conductance obeys $I_{\text{K}} = \bar{g}_{\text{K}} n^4 (v_2 - E_{\text{K}})$ with gating kinetics:

$$\frac{dn}{dt} = \alpha_n(v)(1 - n) - \beta_n(v) n$$

$$\alpha_n(v) = \frac{0.01 (v_{\text{mV}} + 55.0)}{1.0 - \exp(-(v_{\text{mV}} + 55.0) / 10.0)} \times 10^3\,\text{s}^{-1}$$
$$\beta_n(v) = 0.125 \exp(-(v_{\text{mV}} + 65.0) / 80.0) \times 10^3\,\text{s}^{-1}$$

where $v_{\text{mV}} = v_{m,2} \times 10^3$. Under direct stimulation, the isolated classical C-fiber conducts at **0.41 m/s**.

In Phase 2, the generic Na⁺ current is replaced with literature-derived TTX-R Nav1.8 and Nav1.9 nociceptor kinetics (Section 2.5).

### 2.3 Extracellular Coupling, Regularization, and Exact Algebraic Elimination

The longitudinal extracellular cleft resistance per unit length $r_e = \rho_e / A_{\text{eff}}$ ($\Omega/\text{m}$) is determined by extracellular resistivity ($\rho_e = 100\,\Omega\cdot\text{cm} = 1.0\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$) and the annular cleft area $A_{\text{eff}}$:

$$A_{\text{eff}} = \pi \left[ \left( \frac{d_1 + d_2}{2} + w_{\text{cleft}} \right)^2 - \left(\frac{d_1}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{d_2}{2}\right)^2 \right]$$

In the zero-cleft limit ($w_{\text{cleft}} \to 0$), $A_{\text{eff}}$ approaches $A_{\text{eff,min}} = \pi d_1 d_2 / 2 \approx 15.7\,\mu\text{m}^2 = 1.57 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{m}^2$, bounding $r_e$ at an upper ceiling $r_{e,\text{max}} = \rho_e / A_{\text{eff,min}} \approx 6.37 \times 10^{10}\,\Omega/\text{m}$. For the nominal restricted cleft width $w_{\text{cleft}} = 20$ nm, $A_{\text{eff}} \approx 16.4\,\mu\text{m}^2$ ($1.64 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{m}^2$) and $r_e \approx 6.10 \times 10^{10}\,\Omega/\text{m}$.

To avoid numerical stiffness associated with the explicit evaluation of membrane capacitive displacement currents, we implement an algebraic elimination that incorporates the capacitive, ionic, and applied-current terms through the membrane equations while removing their explicit appearance from the extracellular solve. From core-conductor theory, total transmembrane current per unit length exiting axon $k$ equals the axial current divergence:

$$i_{m,k}(z,t) = \frac{1}{r_{i,k}} \frac{\partial^2 u_{i,k}}{\partial z^2} = \frac{1}{r_{i,k}} \left( \frac{\partial^2 v_{m,k}}{\partial z^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u_e}{\partial z^2} \right)$$

where $r_{i,k} = 4 \rho_i / (\pi d_{\text{axon},k}^2)$ is the intracellular longitudinal core resistance per unit length ($\Omega/\text{m}$). Extracellular current conservation per unit length requires:

$$\frac{1}{r_e} \frac{\partial^2 u_e}{\partial z^2} - G_e u_e + \sum_{k} i_{m,k} = 0$$

where $G_e = \kappa / r_e$ ($\text{S/m}$) represents weak ohmic leakage from the restricted cleft to the surrounding bulk interstitial tissue, and $\kappa$ ($\text{m}^{-2}$) is a phenomenological regularization parameter. Substituting $i_{m,k}$ yields:

$$\left( \frac{1}{r_e} + \sum_{k} \frac{1}{r_{i,k}} \right) \frac{\partial^2 u_e}{\partial z^2} - \frac{\kappa}{r_e} u_e = - \sum_{k} \frac{1}{r_{i,k}} \frac{\partial^2 v_{m,k}}{\partial z^2}$$

Dividing by the total core conductance $\Sigma_G = \frac{1}{r_e} + \sum_k \frac{1}{r_{i,k}}$ produces a regularized Poisson/Helmholtz equation:

$$\frac{\partial^2 u_e}{\partial z^2} - \kappa_{\text{eff}} u_e = - \frac{1}{\Sigma_G} \sum_{k} \frac{1}{r_{i,k}} \frac{\partial^2 v_{m,k}}{\partial z^2}$$

The algebraic elimination removes the explicit capacitive-current feedback from the extracellular solve, avoiding that particular stiffness pathway; numerical stability was assessed empirically through the reported timestep/convergence tests.

where the effective regularization parameter $\kappa_{\text{eff}}$ ($\text{m}^{-2}$) is:

$$\kappa_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\kappa / r_e}{\Sigma_G}$$

We treat $\kappa = 1.0 \times 10^9\,\text{m}^{-2}$ as a fixed phenomenological model parameter throughout all comparative analyses, defining a nominal pre-elimination spatial decay scale $\lambda_\kappa = 1/\sqrt{\kappa} \approx 31.62\,\mu\text{m}$. It is not an experimentally measured constant. Importantly, the effective spatial decay scale of the reduced Helmholtz operator is governed by $\lambda_{\text{eff}} = 1/\sqrt{\kappa_{\text{eff}}}$, where $\kappa_{\text{eff}} = (\kappa / r_e) / \Sigma_G$. Because total core conductance $\Sigma_G(n)$ expands with fiber count $n$, $\kappa_{\text{eff}}$ decreases and $\lambda_{\text{eff}}$ correspondingly lengthens: $\lambda_{\text{eff}} = 73.3\,\mu\text{m}$ for $n=1$, $209.7\,\mu\text{m}$ for $n=10$, $329.1\,\mu\text{m}$ for $n=25$, and $464.3\,\mu\text{m}$ for $n=50$ (Table 4).

#### Discrete Current Conservation and Operator-Splitting Residual Analysis
To evaluate current conservation rigorously, we explicitly distinguish between two separate conservation metrics:
1. **Discrete Algebraic Operator Identity Residual ($R_{\text{alg}}$):** Evaluates whether the banded matrix solve numerically satisfies the discretized Helmholtz operator:
$$R_{\text{alg}}(z_j, t^{n+1}) = \left| \left( \frac{1}{r_e} + \sum_k \frac{1}{r_{i,k}} \right) \nabla^2 u_e^{n+1}(z_j) - \frac{\kappa}{r_e} u_e^{n+1}(z_j) + \sum_k \frac{1}{r_{i,k}} \nabla^2 v_k^n(z_j) \right|$$
Across all spatial compartments and timesteps, the discrete operator identity is satisfied to machine precision: $\max |R_{\text{alg}}| \approx 1.95 \times 10^{-17}\,\text{A/m}$ ($1.3 \times 10^{-12}\%$ of peak transmembrane source current, $\sim 1.49 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{A/m}$).
2. **Physical Dynamic Current-Balance Residual ($R_{\text{phys}}$):** Evaluates the physical Kirchhoff current balance using the actual updated transmembrane currents $i_{m,k}^{\text{phys}}(z_j, t^{n+1}) = \pi d_{\text{axon},k} \left[ C_{m,k} \frac{v_k^{n+1} - v_k^n}{\Delta t} + I_{\text{ion},k}(v_k^n, \mathbf{w}_k^{n+1}) - I_{\text{stim},k}^{n+1} \right]$:
$$R_{\text{phys}}(z_j, t^{n+1}) = \left| \frac{1}{r_e} \nabla^2 u_e^{n+1}(z_j) - \frac{\kappa}{r_e} u_e^{n+1}(z_j) + \sum_k i_{m,k}^{\text{phys}}(z_j, t^{n+1}) \right|$$
Because linear axial diffusion is stepped implicitly while $u_e^{n+1}$ is computed from the explicit state $v_k^n$, the physical current balance exhibits an implicit–explicit operator-splitting lag:
$$L_{\text{split}}(z_j, t^{n+1}) = \sum_k \frac{1}{r_{i,k}} \nabla^2 (v_k^{n+1} - v_k^n) = O(\Delta t)$$
Under systematic temporal refinement across $\Delta t \in [5.0, 2.5, 1.25, 0.625, 0.3125, 0.15625]\,\mu\text{s}$ at fixed $\Delta z = 10\,\mu\text{m}$, and the benchmark-default cleft width of 100 nm, the peak physical residual converges monotonically toward zero:
- $\Delta t = 5.0\,\mu\text{s}$: $\max |R_{\text{phys}}| = 3.88 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{A/m}$ ($26.20\%$ of peak source)
- $\Delta t = 2.5\,\mu\text{s}$ (nominal production step): $\max |R_{\text{phys}}| = 3.24 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{A/m}$ ($21.78\%$)
- $\Delta t = 1.25\,\mu\text{s}$: $\max |R_{\text{phys}}| = 2.59 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{A/m}$ ($17.35\%$)
- $\Delta t = 0.625\,\mu\text{s}$: $\max |R_{\text{phys}}| = 1.82 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{A/m}$ ($12.19\%$)
- $\Delta t = 0.3125\,\mu\text{s}$: $\max |R_{\text{phys}}| = 1.15 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{A/m}$ ($7.70\%$)
- $\Delta t = 0.15625\,\mu\text{s}$: $\max |R_{\text{phys}}| = 6.65 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{A/m}$ ($4.44\%$)

The apparent local convergence order rises from $p = 0.26$ at coarse steps to $p = 0.79$ at the finest step pair (`results/convergence/kcl_refinement_study.csv`), and is still increasing at the smallest step; the data are consistent with, but do not by themselves demonstrate, first-order asymptotic convergence of the IMEX splitting lag.

### 2.4 Numerical Scheme and Convergence Methodology

The system is integrated using an implicit–explicit (IMEX) operator-splitting scheme on a spatial grid of nominal $\Delta z = 10\,\mu\text{m}$ ($N = 1000$ compartments) with time step $\Delta t = 2.5\,\mu\text{s}$. Linear axial diffusion terms are stepped implicitly via backward Euler using tridiagonal LAPACK solvers (`solve_banded`), while nonlinear ionic currents are updated explicitly. Extracellular potential $u_e$ is obtained at each step by solving the banded Helmholtz operator.

#### 2.4.1 Spatial Refinement Study
To verify spatial convergence, $\Delta z$ was refined across $[20, 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, 1.0]\,\mu\text{m}$ holding all physical dimensions, including $l_{\text{node}} = 1.0\,\mu\text{m}$, strictly fixed, with the temporal step held constant at $\Delta t = 2.5\,\mu\text{s}$ and the cleft width at $w_{\text{cleft}} = 20$ nm (`src/run_convergence_battery.py`). As detailed in Section 3.1 and Table 5, the isolated Aβ conduction velocity (**41.03 m/s**) and the action-potential excursion at $z = 4$ mm (**80.44 mV**, an absolute peak of $+0.44$ mV from rest $-80.0$ mV) were identical at every grid step. The coupled responses converged monotonically: the single-pair $\Delta V$ ($n=1$) rose from $1.237$ mV at $20\,\mu\text{m}$ to $1.354$ mV at $1.0\,\mu\text{m}$, and the multi-fiber $\Delta V$ ($n=25$) from $5.919$ mV to $6.050$ mV, with successive changes below $0.5\%$ for $\Delta z \le 2.5\,\mu\text{m}$. The nominal grid ($\Delta z = 10\,\mu\text{m}$) underestimates the finest-grid values by $2.1\%$ ($n=1$) and $0.6\%$ ($n=25$).

#### 2.4.2 Temporal Refinement Study
Temporal convergence was evaluated independently across $\Delta t \in [5.0, 2.5, 1.25, 0.625, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125]\,\mu\text{s}$ (holding $\Delta z = 10.0\,\mu\text{m}$ and $w_{\text{cleft}} = 20$ nm fixed). Single-pair and multi-fiber event classifications (`LOCAL_STIMULUS_TRANSIENT`) remain unchanged across all time steps. Aβ conduction velocity depends weakly on the time step: it decreases from $42.11\,\text{m/s}$ at $5.0\,\mu\text{s}$ to $41.03\,\text{m/s}$ at the nominal $2.5\,\mu\text{s}$ and $40.00\,\text{m/s}$ at $1.25\,\mu\text{s}$, then changes by less than $0.3\%$ per halving ($39.80\,\text{m/s}$ at $0.125\,\mu\text{s}$). However, as documented in Section 4.6, the absolute peak amplitude of the single-pulse multi-fiber ($n=25$) response exhibits notable timestep dependence: $6.015\,\text{mV}$ at $\Delta t = 2.5\,\mu\text{s}$, $8.673\,\text{mV}$ at $1.25\,\mu\text{s}$, $11.557\,\text{mV}$ at $0.625\,\mu\text{s}$, $12.424\,\text{mV}$ at $0.5\,\mu\text{s}$, $14.470\,\text{mV}$ at $0.25\,\mu\text{s}$, and $15.313\,\text{mV}$ at $0.125\,\mu\text{s}$ (`results/convergence/temporal_convergence_final.csv`). Richardson extrapolation across the finest dyadic steps ($\Delta t \in [0.5, 0.25, 0.125]\,\mu\text{s}$, apparent order $p \approx 1.28$) yields an asymptotic estimate of $\approx 15.90\,\text{mV}$ (or $\approx 16.16\,\text{mV}$ under first-order extrapolation, $p = 1.0$). We explicitly emphasize that these extrapolated values are mathematical estimates rather than directly simulated converged points; thus, while event classification remains robust and invariant across all tested timesteps, single-pulse potential magnitude is not quantitatively converged.

#### 2.4.3 Independent Reference Solver Validation for Aβ Fiber
To verify the numerical implementation of the saltatory Aβ axon model against an independent integration scheme, a standalone reference solver was implemented (`audit_reference_solver.py`). It integrates the axial diffusion term with Crank-Nicolson and updates the CRRSS gates with a Rush-Larsen step at the midpoint voltage, treating ionic and stimulus currents explicitly as in the production solver. Evaluated at $\Delta t = 0.625\,\mu\text{s}$ and nominal $\Delta z = 10\,\mu\text{m}$, with a 2-nA, 0.2-ms stimulus at node 0, the production IMEX solver yielded an Aβ conduction velocity of $40.00\,\text{m/s}$ compared to $39.51\,\text{m/s}$ for the Crank-Nicolson reference solver (a $1.2\%$ difference; the action potential excursion at $z = 4$ mm differed by $0.24\%$, $79.98\,\text{mV}$ vs $79.78\,\text{mV}$). This agreement supports the numerical integration of the Aβ cable dynamics specifically; the two solvers share the membrane model, geometry, and explicit ionic-current treatment, so the comparison does not constitute a full reference solver for the coupled multi-fiber system.

### 2.5 Nav1.8/Nav1.9 Nociceptor Kinetics (Phase 2)

Phase 2 replaces the classical HH Na⁺ current with TTX-R Nav1.8 and Nav1.9 currents (Akopian et al., 1996; Cummins et al., 1999; Klein et al., 2017):

$$I_{\text{ion},2} = \bar{g}_{18} m_8^3 h_8 (v_2 - E_{\text{Na}}) + \bar{g}_{19} m_9 (v_2 - E_{\text{Na}}) + \bar{g}_{\text{K}} n^4 (v_2 - E_{\text{K}}) + g_{\text{leak}} (v_2 - E_{\text{leak},2})$$

$$m_{8,\infty}(v) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(v + 25.0) / 6.0)}, \quad \tau_{m8} = 1.5\,\text{ms}$$
$$h_{8,\infty}(v) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp((v + 42.0) / 6.0)}, \quad \tau_{h8} = 2.0\,\text{ms}$$
$$m_{9,\infty}(v) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(v + 50.0) / 5.0)}, \quad \tau_{m9} = 10.0\,\text{ms}$$

The delayed-rectifier $\text{K}^+$ gating variable $n$ and its voltage-dependent kinetics are retained directly from the classical Hodgkin-Huxley formulation described in Section 2.2.2 (with delayed-rectifier conductance calibrated to $\bar{g}_{\text{K}} = 60.0\,\text{mS/cm}^2$ and $E_{\text{K}} = -77.0$ mV). Nav1.8 inactivation kinetics were calibrated ($V_{1/2} = -42.0$ mV, $k = 6.0$ mV, $\tau_{h8} = 2.0$ ms) to eliminate a non-repolarizing attractor and ensure physiological action potential duration (5–10 ms). Membrane parameters are $\bar{g}_{18} = 2000\,\text{mS/cm}^2$, $\bar{g}_{19} = 0.2\,\text{mS/cm}^2$, $E_{\text{Na}} = +50.0$ mV, and $E_{\text{leak},2} = -55.0$ mV. To eliminate initialization transients, a 20-ms pre-stimulus settling simulation is performed prior to stimulus onset (`get_settled_c_fiber`), establishing a stable resting baseline at **$-66.82$ mV** (drift $< 10\,\mu\text{V}$ over 20 ms; steady-state gating variables $m_8 = 0.0009$, $h_8 = 0.9843$, $m_9 = 0.0334$, $n = 0.2902$).

### 2.6 Multi-Fiber Bundle Formulation and Temporal Jitter

In intact peripheral nerves, unmyelinated axons are enclosed within Schwann cell cytoplasm inside Remak bundles (Murinson & Griffin, 2004), isolated from myelinated Aβ fibers. The present geometry models a **pathologically altered or confined lesion space** (e.g., focal compression or demyelination) where denuded axolemmas share a restricted interstitial cleft ($w_{\text{cleft}} = 20$ nm).

To evaluate spatial summation, we extend the system to $n$ synchronized Aβ fibers sharing the cleft with one C-fiber. Under synchronous stimulation, identical fibers contribute symmetrically. We model a single representative Aβ cable and scale its current source by $n$. Total core conductance scales as:

$$\Sigma_G(n) = \frac{1}{r_e} + \frac{n}{r_{i,1}} + \frac{1}{r_{i,2}}$$

To assess the impact of desynchronization, we implement an asynchronous recruitment protocol where Aβ activation times are distributed across a uniform jitter window of $1.5$ ms ($\pm 0.75$ ms) discretized into $K = 21$ temporal phases:

$$t_{\text{stim},k} = t_{\text{pulse}} + \delta t_k, \quad \delta t_k \in [-0.75, +0.75]\,\text{ms}$$

The ensemble source term is averaged across all $K$ phases before driving the extracellular Helmholtz solve.

---

### 2.7 Operational Detection Endpoints

To maintain strict objectivity, every simulation is classified automatically by an integrated spatiotemporal waveform analyzer (`classify_waveform` in `src/phase1_classical_hh/spike_detector.py`):
1. `NO_RESPONSE`: Peak downstream depolarization $\Delta V < 0.5$ mV.
2. `PASSIVE_DEPOLARIZATION`: Downstream $\Delta V < 45$ mV or maximum rate of rise $\max(dV/dt) < 30$ V/s.
3. `LOCAL_STIMULUS_TRANSIENT`: Depolarization exceeds threshold proximal to the electrode ($z < 2.0$ mm) but decays below threshold in the downstream cable window ($z \in [3.0, 8.0]$ mm).
4. `LOCAL_ABORTED_SPIKE`: Threshold ($-20$ mV) crossed at $z = 4.0$ mm but fails to propagate past $z = 8.0$ mm.
5. `PROPAGATED_ACTION_POTENTIAL`: Threshold crossed sequentially at both $z = 4.0$ mm and $z = 8.0$ mm with conduction velocity within physiological bounds ($0.2 \le \text{CV} \le 3.0$ m/s for C-fibers).

### 2.8 Uniform Hodgkin-Huxley Kinetics Shift (Phase 1 Sensitivity Protocol)

To explore whether classical Hodgkin-Huxley dynamics could support a quiescent yet excitable sensitized state near threshold, an artificial parameter sensitivity perturbation was applied to the Phase 1 C-fiber model. The voltage-dependent rate functions $\alpha(v)$ and $\beta(v)$ for sodium and potassium gating were subjected to a uniform hyperpolarizing shift $\Delta V_{\text{shift}}$:

$$\tilde{\alpha}_x(v) = \alpha_x(v + \Delta V_{\text{shift}}), \quad \tilde{\beta}_x(v) = \beta_x(v + \Delta V_{\text{shift}}) \quad (x \in \{m, h, n\})$$

This protocol served strictly as a parameter sensitivity exploration rather than a physiological representation of inflammatory sensitization. It evaluated whether shifting the activation window closer to the unperturbed resting potential could enable single-pulse Aβ ephaptic inputs to cross threshold without precipitating runaway spontaneous pacemaking. A positive $\Delta V_{\text{shift}}$ displaces every rate function toward more negative membrane potentials. For both sensitization protocols (Sections 3.3 and 3.4) the C-fiber was started at the exact equilibrium of the unperturbed membrane (-65.13 mV), the perturbation was switched on at t = 0, and the membrane was followed for 1 s. Because a uniform perturbation with a quiescent Aβ fiber leaves the cable spatially uniform, the analysis was carried out on the space-clamped membrane equations (identical rate functions and conductances) and verified on the full coupled cable at selected points (run_sensitization_phase1.py).

### 3.3 Sensitization via Persistent Depolarizing Bias (Phase 1)

Applying a uniform depolarizing bias current to the C-fiber (starting from its exact resting equilibrium, -65.13 mV, with the bias switched on at t = 0; Section 2.8) produced a stable, quiescent sensitized state only up to a bias of ≈ 0.10 A/m², where the baseline rises to -61.9 mV (0.05 A/m²) and -59.8 mV (0.10 A/m²). In this window the marginal ephaptic perturbation evoked by a passing Aβ action potential was unchanged by the bias ($\Delta V$ = 1.33 mV for n = 1 at every bias tested) and failed to reach firing threshold. Above ≈ 0.108 A/m² the C-fiber fired repetitively with no Aβ input (60–96 Hz at 0.11–0.30 A/m²); between 0.4 and ≈ 1.2 A/m² it showed large oscillations (peak-to-peak 69 mV falling to 6 mV) that stayed below 0 mV, and above ≈ 1.23 A/m² it settled on a stable depolarized silent state (-45.0 mV at 1.3 A/m²; depolarization block) (Table 1; sensitization_phase1_bias.csv). Classical HH kinetics therefore offer only a narrow window of stable sensitization (≲ 5.3 mV of depolarization) before intrinsic pacemaking, indicating the absence of a broad stable sensitized intermediate.

### 3.4 Sensitization via Uniform HH Kinetics Shift (Phase 1 Sensitivity Test)

Applying a uniform shift $\Delta V_{\text{shift}}$ to all HH rate functions served as a mathematical sensitivity exploration (Section 2.8). Shifts up to 8.5 mV (and negative shifts down to -20 mV) left the fiber quiescent, with the resting potential moving slightly hyperpolarized (-66.35 mV at $\Delta V_{\text{shift}}$ = 3 mV; -67.83 mV at 8.5 mV) and the Aβ-evoked midpoint perturbation unchanged ($\Delta V$ = 1.33 mV for n = 1; 5.07–5.10 mV for n = 25). At $\Delta V_{\text{shift}}$ ≈ 8.56 mV the fiber switched abruptly into spontaneous pacemaking without external stimulation (≈ 52–58 Hz at 8.6–10 mV; sensitization_phase1_kinetics_shift.csv). The shift therefore did not create a stable, quiescent yet near-threshold sensitized state: the transition from quiescence to pacemaking is abrupt, which motivated Phase 2.

### 3.5 High-Frequency Stimulation, Propagation Timing, and Lesion Boundary Controls

Delivering pulse trains at 100 Hz ($n=25, w_{\text{cleft}}=20$ nm) reveals critical spatiotemporal propagation dynamics that distinguish stimulation-boundary artifacts from pure endogenous ephaptic crosstalk:

#### 3.5.1 Full-Length Cable Dynamics and Propagation Latency
In the full-length coupled model ($w_{\text{cleft}} = 20$ nm across the entire 10-mm cable), injecting a 100-nA stimulus into 25 Aβ fibers at the sealed boundary ($z = 0$) generates a large local extracellular voltage transient at the boundary ($u_e \approx +265$ mV). In the Phase 1 classical Hodgkin-Huxley model, this transient initiates a slow C-fiber action potential at $t = 0.02$ ms. 
Because unmyelinated C-fiber conduction velocity is $0.41$ mm/ms, this action potential requires:
- **$12.90$ ms** to propagate from $z = 0$ to the cable midpoint ($z = 5.0$ mm), and
- **$20.16$ ms** to reach the downstream recording electrode ($z = 8.0$ mm).

This propagation latency explains key reporting differences across historical simulation runs:
1. **Short Observation Windows ($T \le 5.2$ ms):** In single-pulse simulations evaluated over $5.2$ ms (e.g., `EXP_E02_E03_reconciliation.csv` and `phase2_temporal_summation.csv`), the traveling wave has only propagated to $z \approx 1.8$ mm. Downstream recording sites ($z \ge 3.0$ mm) record only the rapid passing Aβ wave at $t \approx 0.25$ ms ($+5.039$ mV at midpoint, $+6.015$ mV downstream maximum at $z = 7.0$ mm). Consequently, the spike classifier correctly classifies the downstream trace as `LOCAL_STIMULUS_TRANSIENT` with `c_propagated_ap = False`.
2. **Extended Observation Windows ($T \ge 30$ ms):** When the single-pulse simulation is extended to $T = 30$ ms (`final_100Hz_reconciliation.csv`, row 0), the slow wave initiated at $t=0$ completes its traverse, reaching the downstream electrode at $t = 20.16$ ms ($V_2 = +24.74$ mV, `spiked = True`).
3. **100-Hz Pulse Train Progression:** In a 100-Hz train (inter-pulse interval $10$ ms), Pulse 2 is delivered at $t = 10$ ms. The traveling spike initiated at $t=0$ reaches the midpoint at $t = 12.90$ ms—within the Pulse 2 observation window ($[10, 20]$ ms)—producing a midpoint peak of $+24.83$ mV. In historical exploratory runs truncated at $T = 15.2$ ms, the wave was captured in flight at $z \approx 2.1$ mm before reaching the downstream electrode ($z = 8.0$ mm), producing an artificial `LOCAL_ABORTED_SPIKE` classification. With adequate observation time ($T \ge 35$ ms), fully propagated action potentials are recorded downstream (midpoint peak $+24.83$ mV, downstream peak $+24.70$ mV at $8.0$ mm, $\text{CV} = 0.41$ m/s; `PROPAGATED_ACTION_POTENTIAL`, spiked = True; Table 1).
4. **State-Reset Mechanism Control:** Resetting the C-fiber membrane potential and gating variables to rest immediately prior to Pulse 2 ($t = 10$ ms; `run_test3_mechanism.py`) physically extinguishes the traveling wave in flight at $z \approx 4.0$ mm, completely preventing downstream spike arrival. This confirms that downstream threshold crossing in repetitive trains requires uninterrupted continuous membrane-state propagation along the unmyelinated cable.

#### 3.5.2 Grounded Stimulation Site and Interior Lesion Control
To determine whether ephaptic threshold crossing can occur purely from endogenous Aβ action potentials traveling through a pathological lesion without any boundary stimulus coupling, an interior lesion control experiment was implemented (`src/interior_lesion_test.py` and `src/run_lesion_audit.py`). In this control:
- The stimulation site ($z \in [0, 2.0]$ mm) is grounded in bulk interstitial fluid ($r_e = 0, u_e = 0$).
- The restricted pathological cleft ($w_{\text{cleft}} = 20$–$50$ nm) is strictly confined to an interior lesion zone ($z \in [3.0, 7.0]$ mm).
- Aβ action potentials are initiated in the grounded zone and propagate endogenously into the lesion.

Under these conditions, pure endogenous ephaptic crosstalk produced peak C-fiber depolarizations of only 1.33 mV in Phase 1 (single Aβ fiber, 20-nm cleft; peak -63.67 mV against a -65.0 mV rest) and 9.48 mV in Phase 2 ($n=25$, 20-nm cleft; peak -57.41 mV) inside the lesion. **No action potential was evoked in the C-fiber in either phase.** In Phase 1 the lesion peak was identical for 5, 10 and 20 pulses at 800 Hz, and in Phase 2 a 10-pulse 800-Hz train gave the same -57.41 mV peak as a single pulse, so no pulse-to-pulse build-up was observed (run_lesion_audit.py); 20-nm and 50-nm clefts differed by about 0.1 mV or less. Peak depolarization therefore remained strictly subthreshold in this grounded configuration. The control covers one geometry (grounded stimulation site, lesion at 3–7 mm, 20–50 nm clefts, 800-Hz trains) and does not exclude threshold crossing under other conditions.

#### 3.5.3 Threshold Resistance of Nav1.8/Nav1.9 Nociceptors (Phase 2)
Furthermore, in the Phase 2 nociceptor model incorporating realistic TTX-resistant Nav1.8 and Nav1.9 kinetics, the higher activation threshold of Nav1.8 ($-35.0$ mV vs $-55.0$ mV for classical HH Na⁺) renders the C-fiber resistant to threshold crossing at moderate recruitment (n ≤ 28; at n ≥ 29 the ungrounded boundary transient can launch a spike, Section 3.9.1):
- Even in the ungrounded full-length cleft with $n = 25$ synchronized Aβ fibers and extended $40$-ms simulation time, the Phase 2 C-fiber reaches a midpoint peak of only **$-56.66$ mV** ($\Delta V = 10.16$ mV above resting baseline $-66.82$ mV; `phase2_navc_multifiber.csv`).
- The peak remains **$21.66$ mV below the Nav1.8 threshold** (-35.0 mV), and in the single-pulse and n ≤ 28 conditions of Fig. 7 the C-fiber produces `c_propagated_ap = False` and `spiked = False`. A 40-ms run at n = 28 gives the same result (midpoint peak -56.51 mV; no C-fiber voltage above -58.8 mV at z ≥ 0.3 mm after 1.5 ms).

### 3.9 Multi-Fiber Bundle Spatial Summation, Temporal Jitter, and Mechanism Control

#### 3.9.1 Multi-Fiber Spatial Summation
In the Phase 1 classical HH model, sweeping synchronized Aβ fiber count $n \in [1, 50]$ in a $20$ nm cleft ($V_{\text{rest}} = -65.000$ mV) produced a progressive increase in subthreshold depolarization: from $\Delta V = 1.325$ mV at $n = 1$ to $\Delta V = 5.039$ mV at midpoint (and $6.015$ mV downstream maximum) at $n = 25$ (Table 4). As $n$ increased further across the tested range up to $50$, depolarization exhibited saturation-like behavior, leveling off at $\Delta V = 6.184$ mV. This plateau is directly attributable to the expanding parallel intracellular core conductance $\Sigma_G(n)$, which shunts the extracellular cleft. Concurrently, collective cleft loading decelerates Aβ conduction velocity from $40.0$ m/s ($n = 1$) to **32.00 m/s** ($n = 25$) and $27.1$ m/s ($n = 50$). Across the entire tested range of $n \le 50$, single-pulse multi-fiber spatial summation remains strictly subthreshold.

In the Phase 2 Nav1.8/Nav1.9 nociceptor model ($V_{\text{rest}} = -66.82$ mV; Fig. 4), synchronous multi-fiber activation similarly produces an asymptotic subthreshold depolarization trend, rising from a midpoint peak of $-65.07$ mV ($\Delta V = 1.76$ mV) at $n = 1$ to $-56.66$ mV ($\Delta V = 10.16$ mV) at $n = 25$, and saturating near $-56.40$ mV ($\Delta V = 10.42$ mV) at $n = 50$, remaining well below the nociceptor threshold ($-35.0$ mV).

The midpoint values above are read within the first 3–5 ms after the pulse. In the ungrounded full-length model, the same boundary transient that produces the 100-Hz result (Section 3.5.1) can also launch a slow C-fiber action potential at the sealed boundary z = 0 once enough Aβ fibers are summed: this occurs for n ≥ 2 in Phase 1 (n = 1 does not fire) and for n ≥ 29 in Phase 2 (threshold n* ≈ 28.5; boundary_spike_phase1.csv, boundary_spike_phase2.csv). The spike conducts at 0.41 m/s (Phase 1) or 0.26 m/s (Phase 2) and reaches the midpoint only after roughly 13 ms (Phase 1) or 20 ms (Phase 2), so it lies outside the midpoint window and is not part of the saturation trend. In the grounded interior-lesion control no action potential occurred for any n up to 200 (Phase 2, 20 nm cleft: lesion-zone peak -56.68 mV at n = 50 and -58.49 mV at n = 200; lesion_control_high_n.csv).

#### 3.9.2 Temporal Dispersion / Jitter Attenuation
Introducing a modeled $1.5$ ms temporal onset-dispersion window across the Aβ population desynchronized the source currents, reducing temporal overlap and sharply attenuating depolarization. In the Phase 1 model at $n = 25$, peak downstream depolarization is suppressed from $6.015$ mV to **0.426 mV**—a **92.9% attenuation**. In the Phase 2 model at $n = 25$ (Fig. 4), peak midpoint potential is suppressed from $-56.66$ mV ($\Delta V = 10.16$ mV) to **$-66.20$ mV** ($\Delta V = 0.63$ mV above rest $-66.82$ mV)—a **93.8% attenuation**. This establishes that multi-fiber spatial summation is highly sensitive to input synchrony under tested conditions across both channel models.

Peak absolute C-fiber midpoint membrane potential ($V_2$ at $z = 5.0$ mm) is plotted across synchronized Aβ fiber count $n \in [1, 50]$ under synchronous recruitment (jitter $0.0$ ms; peaking at $-65.07$ mV for $n=1$, $-58.84$ mV for $n=10$, $-56.66$ mV for $n=25$, and $-56.40$ mV for $n=50$, demonstrating an asymptotic subthreshold trend) and under a modeled $1.5$-ms onset-dispersion window (jitter $1.5$ ms across $K=21$ phases; peaking at $-66.20$ mV for $n=25$, representing $\Delta V = 0.63$ mV above rest and a $93.8\%$ attenuation of ephaptic depolarization). #### 3.9.3 Mechanism Control: Membrane State vs Extracellular Potential Accumulation
To determine whether repeated 100-Hz events are driven by extracellular potential buildup or by C-fiber membrane-state accumulation, we executed a controlled state-reset experiment (run_test3_mechanism.py). In the control simulation, extracellular potential pulses $u_e$ returned to baseline between pulses, with no progressive accumulation. Resetting the C-fiber membrane potential and gating variables to rest immediately before Pulse 2 ($t = 10$ ms) extinguished the traveling wave in flight, so no downstream spike was recorded. This shows that the downstream spike depends on uninterrupted propagation of a wave already underway; because a reset removes a wave in flight by construction, it does not by itself separate membrane-state accumulation from conduction delay. The stronger evidence is the single-pulse run, which reproduces the same downstream spike when observed for $T \ge 30$ ms (Table 1). Together, these results indicate that the events recorded in the Pulse 2 window are delayed propagation of a spike initiated by Pulse 1 at the sealed boundary, not demonstrated pulse-to-pulse recruitment.

## 4 Discussion

### 4.1 Principal Findings
This study provides a rigorous, closed-loop computational evaluation of bidirectional ephaptic crosstalk between myelinated Aβ afferents and unmyelinated C-fibers. Across both classical HH and nociceptor-realistic Nav1.8/Nav1.9 channel kinetics, our simulations demonstrate:
1. **Single-pair coupling under single-pulse stimulation is insufficient under tested conditions** to evoke an action potential across the tested cleft-width range of $20$ nm to $5\,\mu\text{m}$.
2. In the Phase 1 classical-HH model, synchronized multi-fiber spatial summation produces substantial subthreshold depolarization ($+5.04$ to $+6.01$ mV for $n=25$), but plateaus asymptotically near $+6.2$ mV due to parallel axoplasmic core conductance shunting. The Phase 2 Nav1.8/Nav1.9 model shows the same qualitative saturation, reaching $+10.16$ mV at $n=25$ and $+10.42$ mV at $n=50$.
3. **Temporal dispersion strongly attenuates spatial summation**, reducing peak ephaptic drive by $92.9\%$ in Phase 1 ($93.8\%$ in Phase 2) under the modeled $1.5$-ms onset-dispersion window.
4. **Repetitive 100-Hz stimulation did not demonstrate temporal summation to threshold.** In the ungrounded model, the propagated spike recorded in the Pulse 2 window is initiated by Pulse 1 at the sealed stimulation boundary and delayed by slow conduction (0.41 m/s); in the grounded interior-lesion control, crosstalk remained subthreshold in every configuration tested.

---

### 4.2 Single-Pair Coupling Insufficiency
Single-pair ephaptic drive generates peak membrane perturbations of only $\approx$ 1.3 mV in a 20-nm cleft, substantially below the depolarization required to initiate regenerative firing under the tested Phase 1 model. Within the present core-conductor formulation, the single-fiber source produces only limited current transfer into the shared extracellular pathway. This finding holds across generic and nociceptor-specific kinetics.

---

### 4.3 Multi-Fiber Spatial Summation and Asymptotic Saturation
In the Phase 1 classical-HH model, extending the system to $25$ synchronized Aβ fibers generates an approximately $6.0$-mV depolarization ($5.039$ mV midpoint, $6.015$ mV downstream maximum). However, the response saturates asymptotically near $+6.2$ mV. The Phase 2 Nav1.8/Nav1.9 model reproduces the same saturation mechanism qualitatively, reaching a midpoint depolarization of $10.16$ mV at $n=25$ and $10.42$ mV at $n=50$. The core-conductor derivation reveals the mathematical mechanism underlying this plateau, which involves three interacting factors: (1) as synchronized fiber count $n$ increases, the total axial core conductance $\Sigma_G(n) = 1/r_e + n/r_{i,1} + 1/r_{i,2}$ expands linearly with each added intracellular channel; (2) this expansion of $\Sigma_G$ directly reduces the effective regularization parameter $\kappa_{\text{eff}}(n) = (\kappa/r_e)/\Sigma_G(n)$, systematically lengthening the effective electrotonic spatial decay scale of the reduced 1-D Helmholtz operator, $\lambda_{\text{eff}} = 1/\sqrt{\kappa_{\text{eff}}}$ (from $73.3\,\mu\text{m}$ at $n=1$ to $464.3\,\mu\text{m}$ at $n=50$; Table 4); and (3) in the reduced extracellular operator, the expanding parallel intracellular conductances act as an intrinsic low-resistance resistive shunt that dissipates return currents longitudinally rather than confining them to the cleft, causing the induced extracellular and transmembrane potentials to plateau asymptotically. Thus, multi-fiber spatial summation alone cannot drive single-pulse suprathreshold activation under the tested geometry.

---

### 4.4 High-Frequency Stimulation, Propagation Timing, and Lesion Boundary Controls
In the ungrounded, full-length coupled model ($w_{\text{cleft}} = 20$ nm from $z = 0$ to 10 mm), the propagated C-fiber action potential recorded in the Pulse 2 window is initiated by Pulse 1 at the sealed boundary. Spatiotemporal analysis reveals that injecting 100 nA into 25 Aβ fibers at the sealed boundary $z = 0$ generates a large initial extracellular transient ($u_e \approx +265$ mV) that initiates a slow C-fiber action potential at $t = 0.02$ ms. Because C-fiber conduction velocity is 0.41 mm/ms, this action potential requires 12.90 ms to reach the midpoint ($z = 5.0$ mm)—falling squarely within the Pulse 2 observation window ([10, 20] ms). A single pulse observed for $T \ge 30$ ms yields the same spike, and the state-reset control shows that it depends on continuous membrane-state propagation along the unmyelinated cable, as resetting the cable at $t = 10$ ms extinguishes the traveling wave in flight at $z \approx 4.0$ mm. These results do not show that Pulse 2 or subthreshold accumulation is required for the spike.

Crucially, when the stimulation site ($z \in [0, 2.0]$ mm) is grounded in bulk fluid ($r_e = 0, u_e = 0$) and cleft restriction is confined to an interior lesion ($z \in [3.0, 7.0]$ mm), **no action potential is ever triggered** across Phase 1, Phase 2, or 800-Hz trains up to 20 pulses (`run_lesion_audit.py`). Furthermore, in Phase 2 with realistic TTX-resistant Nav1.8 and Nav1.9 kinetics, the high threshold of Nav1.8 ($-35.0$ mV) keeps the midpoint response subthreshold at n = 25 even in the ungrounded full-length setup (midpoint peak $-56.66$ mV; $\Delta V = 10.16$ mV; $21.66$ mV subthreshold), and the boundary-initiated spike appears only for n ≥ 29 (versus n ≥ 2 in Phase 1; Section 3.9.1). Pure endogenous ephaptic crosstalk in an interior lesion is therefore strictly subthreshold across both classical and nociceptor-realistic kinetics.

---

### 4.5 Synchrony Dependence
The strong attenuation observed under the modeled $1.5$ ms onset-dispersion window ($92.9\%$ in Phase 1; $93.8\%$ in Phase 2) highlights that multi-fiber spatial summation is highly sensitive to the degree of temporal alignment across fibers. While highly synchronous activation may occur during rapid electrical stimulation or sharp mechanical transients, naturalistic tactile stimuli recruiting dispersed afferent populations would exhibit reduced temporal overlap of source currents, substantially attenuating the ephaptic potential.

---

### 4.6 Numerical and Modeling Limitations
1. **One-Dimensional Approximation:** The 1D core-conductor model longitudinally averages extracellular potential, omitting three-dimensional cleft tortuosity, micro-domains, and non-uniform axon packing. Fully coupled 3D EMI formulations (Jæger & Tveito, 2026) are required to resolve localized field hotspots.
2. **Phenomenological Regularization ($\kappa$):** The parameter $\kappa = 1.0\times 10^9\,\text{m}^{-2}$ represents cleft leakage but is not directly measured experimentally. Furthermore, $\kappa_{\text{eff}}$ inherently depends on fiber count $n$ through $\Sigma_G$.
3. **Temporal Non-Convergence of Single-Pulse $n=25$ Peak:** While event classifications are strictly stable under temporal refinement down to $\Delta t = 0.125\,\mu\text{s}$ (`LOCAL_STIMULUS_TRANSIENT` throughout), the single-pulse $n=25$ peak $\Delta V$ magnitude exhibits notable timestep dependence ($6.015\,\text{mV}$ at $\Delta t = 2.5\,\mu\text{s}$ to $15.313\,\text{mV}$ at $0.125\,\mu\text{s}$, $w_{\text{cleft}} = 20$ nm). Richardson extrapolation across the finest steps yields an asymptotic estimate of $\approx 15.90\,\text{mV}$ (first-order $\approx 16.16\,\text{mV}$), but these are extrapolated estimates rather than directly simulated converged points. Absolute potential magnitudes must therefore be interpreted with caution, whereas threshold-crossing and propagation classifications remain robust.
4. **Boundary Stimulation vs. Interior Lesion Coupling:** In the full-length coupled model, current injection at the sealed boundary $z = 0$ creates a localized extracellular potential transient that can initiate a boundary spike (Phase 1: n ≥ 2; Phase 2: n ≥ 29; Section 3.9.1). When the stimulation site is grounded and cleft restriction is confined to an interior lesion ($z \in [3, 7]$ mm), pure endogenous ephaptic crosstalk remained subthreshold (peak depolarization 1.33 mV in Phase 1 and 9.48 mV in Phase 2; no action potentials, including under 800-Hz trains). This control covers one geometry and a 20–50 nm cleft range, so it does not establish that threshold crossing is impossible in other configurations.
5. **Reduced Multi-Fiber Scaling:** The multi-fiber model uses a 1D synchronized source-scaling approximation rather than an explicit 3D anatomical bundle reconstruction.

---

### 4.7 Biological and Clinical Implications
These computational findings generate testable biophysical hypotheses, reframing peripheral ephaptic interaction as a **subthreshold depolarizing influence** rather than an autonomous single-pulse spark generator under the modeled conditions. In pathological scenarios where nociceptors may already be depolarized by inflammatory mediators, subthreshold ephaptic depolarization from synchronized Aβ input could plausibly bring a nociceptor closer to threshold; the present simulations do not show threshold crossing by this route, and the direct etiology of clinical dynamic mechanical allodynia in vivo remains to be established experimentally.

---

### 4.8 Future Work
Key future directions include: (1) extending the model to higher-dimensional parallel axon networks (Chawla & Morgera, 2024; Reutskiy et al., 2003) and 3D EMI finite-element models (Jæger & Tveito, 2026); (2) driving the bundle with naturalistic human microneurography spike trains; and (3) incorporating detailed kinetic models of Nav1.7 and subthreshold potassium channels (Pelot et al., 2021; Sundt et al., 2015; Tigerholm et al., 2014).

---

## 5 Conclusion

A closed-loop core-conductor model demonstrates that single-pair ephaptic crosstalk under single-pulse stimulation is insufficient to trigger ectopic nociceptor action potentials across both classical HH and nociceptor-realistic Nav1.8/Nav1.9 kinetics. Synchronized multi-fiber spatial summation produces substantial subthreshold depolarization (in Phase 1: +5.04 mV midpoint, +6.01 mV downstream maximum for $n=25$ at the nominal production timestep; in Phase 2: +10.16 mV midpoint depolarization to -56.66 mV) that asymptotically saturates due to core conductance shunting, and is strongly attenuated by temporal dispersion (92.9% attenuation in Phase 1, 93.8% in Phase 2). In the ungrounded full-length model, a 100-Hz train yields a propagated C-fiber action potential during the second pulse interval, but this spike is initiated by the first pulse at the sealed stimulation boundary and delayed by slow conduction ($\approx$ 0.41 m/s); a single pulse reproduces it, and the state-reset control shows that it requires uninterrupted propagation. In a grounded interior-lesion control, crosstalk remained subthreshold in every configuration tested. Within the parameter space explored, ephaptic coupling in restricted nerve clefts therefore produced subthreshold depolarization but did not by itself cross threshold; whether other conditions could do so remains untested.
